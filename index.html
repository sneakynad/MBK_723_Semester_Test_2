1. Quantifying training load J&L ch 8, Cormack and Coutts, pp. 101 to 109 and 113 to 117

Orientation

What

Putting a number on the training dose. External load is the work done. Internal load is what that work cost this athlete.

Why it matters

Adaptation follows the internal response, and the same session costs different athletes different amounts. Without a dose there is nothing to interpret fatigue, recovery or injury against.

Links

Load is the input to section 2 (the response), sets how much section 3 recovery is needed, and non-training stress from section 5 raises internal load for the same work.

1.4 Heart rate methods

Basis: HR rises linearly with VO2 across steady-state submaximal work. Good for endurance intensity. Poor for short sprints, lifting, landing and contact, and weaker in intermittent team sport.

Banister TRIMP

D × ΔHR × e^(b × ΔHR)
ΔHR = (HRex − HRrest) / (HRmax − HRrest)
b = 1.92 men, 1.67 women
The exponential weights high intensity more, mirroring the lactate curve.

Edwards zones

Minutes in each %HRmax zone × weighting.
50-60 = 1, 60-70 = 2, 70-80 = 3, 80-90 = 4, 90-100 = 5

Threshold zones

Below VT, VT to RCP, above RCP. Individualised, but needs valid threshold testing. extra usually credited to Lucia.
Worked examples TRIMP. Male, 60 min, mean HR 155, rest 50, max 190. ΔHR = 105/140 = 0.75. TRIMP = 60 × 0.75 × e^1.44 = 45 × 4.22 = 190 AU. Female, same numbers: 158 AU.
Edwards. 10, 20, 15, 10, 5 min in zones 1 to 5 = 10 + 40 + 45 + 40 + 25 = 160 AU.
Denominators differ. Mean HR 148, rest 50, max 190: fraction of HR reserve = 98/140 = 0.70, fraction of HRmax = 148/190 = 0.78. State which one.
TrapsJ&L print TRIMP without Banister's leading coefficient (extra 0.64 men, 0.86 women). Use the J&L form and never compare scores from different equations. A mean HR hides intervals, because the exponential of the average is not the average of the exponentials. In team sport, individual TRIMP beats a team average.

HR limits: equipment cost, expertise to interpret, signal loss, poor for HIIT and strength work, a badly estimated HRmax shifts every zone extra.

1.6 Weekly load, monotony, strain

Daily load = sum of sessionsWeekly load = sum of 7 daysMonotony = mean daily load ÷ SDStrain = weekly load × monotony
Calculation traps
  • A genuine rest day is 0. A missing entry is not 0.
  • Sample vs population SD changes the answer (week A: 1.14 with sample SD, 1.23 with population). Use the SD given, or state which one.
  • Identical loads every day gives SD = 0, so monotony is undefined.
  • Daily SD hides a hard morning plus easy afternoon. Session-based and daily-based SDs give different values (chapter warning).
  • High monotony means little variation. It does not diagnose anything. extra Foster linked monotony above about 2.0 with illness, not in J&L.

1.9 Load, injury and the ACWR

  • Monitoring only earns its place if load links to injury or performance. Both high and low loads raise injury risk in team sport.
  • "Not all load is equal." High-speed and sprint exposure matters more for injury.
  • Load indices relate to performance in groups, but forecasting an individual is poor. Match exercise intensity is not performance.

ACWR = acute load (last week) ÷ chronic load (mean of last 4 weeks)

Coupling in one exampleWeeks: 1800, 2000, 2200, 2600 AU. Coupled chronic = 2150, ACWR = 1.21. Uncoupled (three earlier weeks only) chronic = 2000, ACWR = 1.30. Same athlete, different answer.

For

Fits training sense: not too much too soon. In international guidelines. More than 130 load-injury studies. Associated with injury.

Against

Mathematical coupling. Rolling averages treat day 27 like yesterday. A ratio adds noise and is not normalised. Poor prediction despite association.

Verdict

Use with caution. Track each athlete's raw load changes plus response measures plus athlete and clinician feedback.
Do not write"ACWR 1.20 means 20% more injury risk." It is a descriptive ratio. J&L describe acute load as the fatigue component and chronic load as the fitness component. extra Critics argue the chronic average is only a history of exposure. extra Gabbett's 0.8 to 1.3 "sweet spot" is not in J&L.

2. Fatigue and well-being J&L ch 8, pp. 103 to 117

Orientation

What

The athlete's response to load and to life: fatigue, well-being and readiness for the next task, and how to measure them well enough to act.

Why it matters

Fatigue is required for adaptation, but unrecovered fatigue slides along a continuum to underperformance. The job is telling a real individual change from measurement noise.

Links

Reads against the dose from section 1. A flag leads to a section 3 recovery or load decision, and often to a section 5 conversation first.

Outcomes to hitExplain the fatigue-recovery paradigm and its relevance to well-being and readiness. Explain and evaluate methods to measure, monitor and manage fatigue and well-being.

2.1 Three words that get mixed up

Subjective fatigue

The athlete's experience of tiredness. Only measurable by self-report, as a trait or a state.

Performance fatigue

Chapter definition: failure to maintain the required force, may include a cognitive component. Measured by a performance test.

Readiness

Current capacity for a specific upcoming task. Ready for technical work can still mean not ready for repeated maximal jumps.

A planned overload block lowers readiness on purpose. Feeling fresh every day does not prove the programme works.

2.3 Where fatigue comes from

Central

Proximal to the neuromuscular junction. Reduced neural drive (voluntary activation).

Peripheral

Distal to the junction. Excitation-contraction coupling and force production in the muscle.

Low-frequency fatigue

After high-intensity, moderate to high force, repetitive eccentric or SSC work. Long lasting. Criterion = percutaneous nerve or muscle stimulation, impractical. Field proxy = CMJ.

In simulated soccer, voluntary activation and peripheral fatigue were already down at half-time and stayed down after the match. In the field you usually cannot tell central from peripheral.

2.6 HRV and heart rate recovery

J&L file these under internal load. They behave as response measures.

HRV

Normal variation in beat-to-beat (R-R) intervals. Parasympathetic lowers HR, sympathetic raises it. Analysed in time domain, frequency domain, nonlinear. Higher HRV at rest generally means positive adaptation.

HRR

After exercise: sympathetic withdrawal + parasympathetic reactivation. Faster = fitter. Slower may mean detraining. May flag the onset of overreaching. Needs a standardised task and posture.

In negative states

Overreaching: sympathetic dominance. NFOR and OTS: sympathetic inhibited, parasympathetic up. So "low is bad" is too simple.
HRR example170 to 140 bpm = 30 beats. Later, 170 to 150 under the same conditions = 20 beats, slower. Follow up, do not diagnose.

extra Standardise: same validated sensor, posture, time of day, recording window and analysis. Note illness, travel, sleep. Read the trend, not one reading. J&L's own points are the last two: context is needed, and reliable HRV in a large squad is a practical problem.

2.9 Is the change real?

Reliability

Repeatability. Error = technical + biological. Lets me say a change is real.

Validity

Measures what it claims. Criterion (field vs lab gold standard), construct and ecological (relates to load and performance in my setting).

Sensitivity

Detects a relevant change. A reliable device can repeat a wrong value. A valid test can miss small fatigue changes.

SWC

Smallest change that matters. Different question from error. Often smaller than the noise. extra commonly 0.2 × between-athlete SD.

extra Typical error = random variation in a reliability study. CV = that error as a % of the mean. ICC = relative reliability, depends on spread between athletes, so a high ICC does not guarantee small individual error.

z = (today − own baseline mean) ÷ own baseline SD  Flag at 1.5 SD (authors' practical threshold, not a health cut-off).

ExampleBaseline wellness 18, 19, 17, 18. Mean 18, SD 0.82. Today 16. z = −2.4. Flag. This handles the athlete who always scores high or low. A player can sit above the squad mean and still be well below their own normal.
  • Baseline must allow a meaningful comparison: in-season scores against a competition-phase baseline, not a flogged preseason week.
  • Acute = this week vs baseline. Chronic = rolling or exponentially weighted average over about a month. Weekly averaging of daily wellness loses resolution.
  • Group view: z or STEN against the squad mean, or against a fixed standard so the reference does not drift.

2.12 Recall

What makes overreaching functional?
J&L: a temporary performance reduction due to overload training. The usual extension is that the outcome decides it: performance rebounds after recovery. Intention does not count.
Central vs peripheral fatigue
Proximal vs distal to the neuromuscular junction. Field tests usually cannot separate them.
Why can unchanged CMJ height hide fatigue?
The athlete takes longer to produce the impulse. FT:CT falls while height holds.
CK time course after a match, and why is it a poor overreaching marker?
Up to 48 h, baseline by 72 h. It reflects acute damage, is highly variable, can be high with no performance loss, and OTS occurs with normal CK.
Cortisol thresholds
Rises above 60% max power for longer than 30 min. Highest on waking, lowest in the evening.
DALDA structure
Part A 9 sources of stress, part B 25 stress-reaction symptoms, rated worse, normal, better than normal.
SWC vs typical error
Practical importance vs measurement noise. A worthwhile change can be smaller than the noise and so be undetectable.
Which measures does table 8.2 rate critical, and which questionable?
Critical: RPE, sRPE load, session volume and intensity, AROMs with valid tools. Questionable: biochemical.

3.5 Cryotherapy chambers

Protocol

WBC: 30 s at −60°C, then 2 to 5 min at −110 to −130°C in underwear, mask, gloves, socks. Partial-body (nitrogen, head out): 2 to 3 min at −100 to −150°C.

Positive

Less soreness and stress vs control, anti-inflammatory cytokine shift, subjective sleep, pain. Skin cools more than CWI.

Negative

No better than placebo. No change in IL-6, cortisol, T:C. Little functional recovery. Core and muscle cooling similar to CWI. Body composition alters the effect.

Verdict: evidence insufficient. CWI does the same job for far less money.

3.7 Massage, stretching, active recovery T&G

Massage

Helps relaxation, mood, perceived soreness at 48 h. Does not increase muscle blood flow, and Wiltshire 2010 found it impaired lactate removal. Performance evidence thin.

Static stretching

No evidence it speeds performance recovery or prevents DOMS. No evidence of harm. May reduce pain sensation. Never force painful end range on a damaged muscle.

Active recovery

Clears lactate faster than rest, but lactate is back to baseline long before the next session (Barnett 2006). Gill 2006: 7 min cycling at about 150 W, CWT and compression all beat passive for CK clearance.
Exam distinctionextra Lactate does not cause DOMS. Standard physiology, consistent with T&G's point that lactate is back to resting levels long before the next session. DOMS follows damaging or unfamiliar exercise and peaks days later. "Flushing lactate" cannot be the mechanism for less next-day soreness. Foam rolling is not proven to break scar tissue or realign fibres. It modulates pain perception and gives short-term range.

4.2 Benefits against risks

Benefits

  • Temperature effects above
  • Movement proficiency, balance, trunk strength over time
  • Team cohesion, engagement
  • A free movement screen and monitoring window
  • Time to fix imbalances and ROM limits

Risks and counter-evidence

  • Karpovich: warmed-up 60 m sprinters had more hamstring injuries
  • Athletes have skipped it and set records. Bench players cool for 45 min
  • Fatigue if too long or intense, overheating in the heat
  • Rapid stretch triggers the stretch reflex
  • Mistimed or overloaded potentiation work
  • Over-competitive games early ("a hamstring playing tag")
  • Unfamiliar eccentric work causes soreness even at low volume
  • Reliance on long rituals "feels fragile"
Balanced verdictShort on time, athletes are probably safe with a minimal warm-up. Those who never warm up properly may not be at great short-term injury risk, but they leave potential untapped. No warm-up guarantees injury prevention, and evidence for a whole programme does not prove each component works.

4.3 Structure: general to specific, slow to game speed

From objective to design
Objective: what does the next task demand?Select components that serve itIntensity builds towards task speedDuration long enough to warm, short enough to avoid fatigue and heat strainSequence general to specific, neural work lastTiming: finish close enough that temperature and potentiation remain

Each decision follows from the one before. A 1RM test, a cold evening match and a youth skills session share the first five minutes of getting warm and then diverge, because the objective differs.

1 RaiseThermogenic work, about 5 min. Non-negotiable.
2 Mobilise and activateThe joints the sport needs, about 5 min. Activation, balance, correctives.
3 RehearseSport directions, skills, building speed.
4 PotentiateA few fast, explosive or loaded efforts, with rest. Finish ready, not tired.

Stages can overlap: a lunge with rotation warms, mobilises and coordinates at once.

  • Plan a week of warm-ups with the care given to strength sessions. Break the script (roll, jog, skip, bands, stretch). Monotony is the enemy.
  • Messy beats choreographed. Push athletes to the uncoordinated outer edges of their athleticism: ball overhead, eyes closed, non-dominant hand, time pressure.
  • Progress to near game speed. Feedback at slow speed differs from feedback at game speed. Walking, running and aqua jogging are mechanically different.
  • Think in 5 min blocks. 15 min × 6 days = 18 blocks, stretching takes 6, leaving 12 for whatever the team lacks.
  • Only given 7 min? Make it look like the start of technical practice. Borrow the coach's drills and add superbands. Best case: the last block flows straight into the coach's first drill.

Constraints-based programming

Design the drill so the constraint forces the pattern, and say less. "Skateboarders improve because of the ground." Examples: two steps to catch a med ball overhead (bracing to set), light snatch into single-leg step-up, hula hoop intervals, wickets, stick overhead while sprinting.

Warm-up as a screen

Put one station in the circuit that exposes the fault and stand there. Box drops to watch for knee valgus. Look for obvious problems only. It does not diagnose tissue or predict injury.

Gamification

Small teams. Use the hatred of losing (fastest retire, the rest go again). Rank, record, publish. Occasional novel challenges. For elite teams with safe technique, competing flat out often beats slow rehearsal.

Individualising

The End of Average: a cockpit built on averages of 4000 pilots fitted none. 3 notecards × slots A, B, C = 9 slots a week: physio's priority, coach's priority, one for the athlete's own goal, for buy-in.

4.5 Stretching: how much and where

  • The link between flexibility and injury prevention is weak. One good multijoint stretch each for shoulder, T-spine, hip, ankle.
  • Combine length with strength: deep barbell RDLs beat a year of toe touches. Strength at end range earns range.
  • Match the day. Acceleration: glutes, T-spine, ankle mobility. Top speed: hip mobility, ankle stability, hamstring activation.
  • Start dynamic stretches small and build, to avoid the stretch reflex. Add crawling and climbing. Throwers need scapular work. Barefoot sometimes. Watch fatigue.
  • Wales football keeps static holds to 20 s or less, then moves on to dynamic work.

R&M Backing for a testing question: intense prolonged static stretching can leave a strength deficit for up to 1 h (Fowles 2000). A warm-up run or practice jumps raised vertical jump 3.4% while static stretching lowered it (Young and Behm 2003). No good evidence that pre-activity stretching prevents injury (Pope 2000, Thacker 2004). Dose, timing and the surrounding warm-up matter, so "static stretching is always harmful" is also wrong.

4.6 Potentiation

Two routes

Overspeed: lighter bat, towing, quick-foot ladder, teaches faster firing. Resisted: weights, harness, recruits more and bigger motor units.

Rules

Stay within ±10% of normal load or mechanics change and transfer is lost. Rest 6 to 12 min. Trained lifters benefit most. High individual variability. Never trial it for the first time on competition day.

Plyometrics

Quick-foot drills beat the skipping rope, since the rope caps the speed. Bursts of about 3 s, long rests.

extra J&L call all of this PAP. Strictly, PAP is an enhanced evoked twitch (myosin light-chain phosphorylation, lasts a few minutes). Better voluntary performance, like a higher jump, is PAPE and also involves temperature and other factors (Blazevich and Babault 2019).

Significant negative life event + positive sport event, obsessiveness, perfectionism, total preparation. Shallow reading: "talent needs trauma". Correct reading: high challenge balanced with appropriate support.

Avoidant attachment

Bowlby. Self-reliant, low trust, low help-seeking, task focused, suppress emotion ("bottle, bottle, BANG"). Drawn to sport, and sport reinforces it. Helps coping, but they dodge conflict, will not ask for help or question things. "I'm fine" on the wellness form may not be.

Understand the person: five questions in place of "what's wrong?"

What has happened to you?How did it affect you?What sense did you make of it?What did you do to cope?What are your strengths?

Ask naturally, not as a script. Unsure they want to talk? Share the dilemma: "I noticed you looked upset earlier and wondered if you wanted to talk about it?" Every interaction matters. Mental health is everybody's business.

5.4 Promoting: four principles (VACS)

V. Value the whole person

Person, not commodity.
  • Director gathers everyone to thank a leaver
  • Mental health screening next to physical screening
  • One-to-ones: current state, warning signs, coping (safety planning)
  • No tools? Ask how they are, mean it, follow up "fine"
  • In testing: explain the purpose, discuss results respectfully, no public shaming over a jump score or skinfolds

A. Autonomy and informed choice

Basic human need. Non-negotiables stay (the swimmer still swims laps), so create choice inside them.
  • Ask at least annually if they still want to be in the programme
  • Full information on time, energy, sacrifice before inducting young talent
  • Choice over downtime, study, where they live
  • Involve athletes in the programme, or explain it and invite feedback

C. Connection

Basic need, optimal dose varies (introverts less). Connection beyond the sport.
  • Mix athletes and staff across sports: career fairs, watching or coaching each other
  • Community appearances as real connection, not a tick-box
  • Injury, travel and deselection isolate people. Keep them included

S. Self-care and recovery

  • Periodise the year for mental health: find pinch points (selection, majors, exams), plan downtime before them
  • Recovery spaces (sleep pods, lounges) kept as well as the gym
  • Leaders model it and talk about their own struggles. Midnight emails and praise for 5 am arrivals teach everyone the opposite
Screening ruleK-10, WHO-5, PHQ-9, GAD-7. Only screen inside a developed strategy with trained staff resourced to interpret and act. Same logic as chapter 8: do not collect what you cannot use. Daily wellness monitoring flags a conversation. Formal screening flags a possible concern. Neither diagnoses.

5.5 Protecting: the stress bucket

Notice it filling (self-awareness)Fit taps early, not when it is nearly fullStop water coming in: say no, prioritise, resolve conflict early
  • Some overspill is normal. The fill, overflow, refill cycle is exhausting.
  • Usual taps vanish on tour (family, friends). Plan alternative taps before travel.
  • Do not only add relaxation exercises while leaving an unreasonable schedule untouched. Fix the inflow as well.
  • Training stress and life stress fill the same bucket. Link to internal load and T:C.
  • Look after yourself before others. The more staff model it, the more athletes copy it.

5.6 Responding to a crisis: eight steps

Rare, but the most anxiety-provoking situations staff face. Rehearse with scenarios: rate concern 1 to 10 and say what you would do.

  1. Self-regulateBreathe. Notice my own reaction. Get ready to listen.
  2. ValidateName the struggle. They did the right thing by speaking.
  3. SummariseDo not fix. Reflect back. Buys time, checks assumptions.
  4. Open questions"Can you tell me more?" Talking often lowers the intensity.
  5. Get helpAny risk to self or others, or unsure: I must tell someone and act. Say why. Safety overrides confidentiality, even if they beg.
  6. Safety planNo immediate risk: what next, how will they look after themselves tonight?
  7. End and contractWhen we next speak, when I am and am not available. Not 24/7.
  8. DebriefGet support for myself.

Mnemonic: Some Very Sad Athletes Get Support, Eventually Debrief.

Safety clarificationsextra When worried, ask directly about suicide. Asking does not plant the idea (NIMH guidance). Immediate danger means emergency services first, not the checklist. Stay with the person if safe. Never promise secrecy. A safety plan is not a "no-harm promise" and does not replace emergency care. Know the local lines: SADAG, Lifeline, and the UP crisis line in the study guide.

5.7 Bonus depth: ch 18 (Kiely). In the study guide, not in the test brief

Adaptive resources are limited and shared across all stressorsThe brain predicts demand and allocates resources (predictive coding)Beliefs and mind-set shift the allocationSo coach communication changes the training response
  • "Zombie idea": load produces predictable, proportionate adaptation. Identical twins on one programme responded very differently.
  • Let-down effect: illness arrives when a long high-alert period ends, such as after a major competition.
  • Placebo and nocebo: sham supplement + positive message improved 30 m sprints, + negative message slowed them. About half the mental benefit of training may be placebo-driven.
  • "Stress is enhancing" mind-set gave better cortisol profiles (Crum). Hotel attendants told their work was exercise got healthier with no behaviour change.
  • Careless communication acts as a nocebo. Explaining why, valuing feedback and leading by example act the other way.

Formula reference Every calculation in the handbook: variables, units, conventions, errors. Practise each in Calculations mode

Session-RPE load

load (AU) = CR10 RPE × duration (min)
Variables
RPE = whole-session rating on the CR10 scale. Duration = minutes this athlete actually completed.
Unit
Arbitrary units (AU). An index of internal load, not energy or mechanical work.
Read it as
Daily load = sum of that day's sessions. Compare within the athlete.
Common errors
Using planned team time. Mixing RPE scales. Averaging two sessions when they should be summed.

Weekly load, monotony, strain

weekly = Σ 7 daily loads  |  monotony = mean daily ÷ SD daily  |  strain = weekly × monotony
Variables
Seven daily loads in AU with rest days as 0. SD of those seven values.
Convention
This handbook and the practice mode use the sample SD (n − 1), as in Excel STDEV.S, unless an SD is supplied. Population SD gives a higher monotony. State which you used.
Units
Monotony has no unit. Strain is in AU.
Read it as
High monotony = little day-to-day variation. Same weekly load with a flatter spread gives more strain.
Common errors
Leaving out the rest day. Treating a missing entry as 0. Dividing by SD = 0. Rounding monotony before calculating strain.

Heart rate fractions

ΔHR = (HRex − HRrest) ÷ (HRmax − HRrest)  |  %HRmax = HRex ÷ HRmax
Variables
HRex = mean exercise HR. All in beats per minute.
Unit
A fraction from 0 to 1, no unit.
Common errors
Using %HRmax where HR reserve is required. TRIMP uses ΔHR, Edwards uses %HRmax.

Banister TRIMP (J&L form)

TRIMP = D × ΔHR × e^(b × ΔHR)
Variables
D = duration in min. ΔHR = fraction of HR reserve. b = 1.92 men, 1.67 women.
Unit
AU.
Read it as
The exponential makes each extra step of intensity count for more, mirroring the lactate curve.
Common errors
Using %HRmax. Forgetting the exponent multiplies ΔHR by b first. Comparing with scores from the original equation that has a 0.64 or 0.86 coefficient extra.

Edwards summated zones

load = Σ (minutes in zone × zone weighting 1 to 5)
Variables
Zones are 10% bands of HRmax from 50% to 100%.
Unit
AU.
Common errors
Counting a boundary value in two zones. Weighting time below 50% HRmax.

Resistance volume load

sets × reps × kg  |  sets × reps × %1RM
Unit
kg for the absolute version, AU for the relative version. Neither is joules, because displacement is not included.
Common errors
Mixing the two versions in one series. Reading equal volume load as equal stress.

Acute:chronic workload ratio

ACWR = last week ÷ mean of last 4 weeks (coupled)
Convention
Coupled: the acute week is inside the chronic mean, as the chapter describes. Uncoupled: chronic = mean of the previous 3 weeks only.
Unit
A ratio, no unit.
Read it as
A description of change in load. It is not a probability of injury.
Common errors
Writing 1.20 as "20% more risk". Not saying which convention was used.

CMJ flight time to contraction time

FT:CT = flight time (s) ÷ contraction time (s)  |  % change = (today − baseline) ÷ baseline × 100
Read it as
A fall with unchanged flight time means the athlete needed longer to produce the jump: a strategy change.
Common errors
Inverting the ratio. Dividing the change by today's value.

Individual standardised score

z = (today − own baseline mean) ÷ own baseline SD
Convention
Sample SD of the athlete's baseline scores. Authors' practical flag: beyond 1.5 SD in the unfavourable direction.
Common errors
Using the squad mean. Ignoring scale direction (is high good or bad?). Treating 1.5 as a health cut-off.

Change against test error

% change = (today − baseline) ÷ baseline × 100, then compare with the test's error (%)
Read it as
Larger than the error: probably a real biological change. Whether it matters is a separate question answered by the smallest worthwhile change.
Common errors
Calling a change inside the noise "fatigue". Treating "real" as "important".

TSS and rTSS are discussed conceptually in the chapter without a calculation protocol, so they have no practice problems here.

8. Practice questions Closed book, typed, 1.5 min per mark. Open the outline only after answering. Outlines are practice material, not an official memorandum

Short, 2 to 6 marks

Define internal load and explain why it is prioritised. (3)
Psychophysiological stress experienced. It determines the adaptive stimulus. Same external work gives different internal load through fitness, nutrition, genetics, psychological state.
Court session 70 min RPE 6, gym 40 min RPE 5. Week: 620, 500, 350, 700, 280, 350, 0 AU, SD given as 216.4. Daily load, weekly load, monotony, strain. (6)
420 + 200 = 620 AU. Weekly 2800, mean 400. Monotony 400 ÷ 216.4 = 1.85. Strain 2800 × 1.85 = about 5180 AU. Interpret against her own norm, not a universal threshold.
Four limitations of HR-based monitoring. (4)
Cost, expertise, signal loss, poor for HIIT, strength and intermittent work, depends on accurate HRmax.
Differentiate FOR, NFOR and OTS. (6)
Planned, short, rebounds. Unplanned, weeks, no payoff. Persists despite big training cuts, psychological disturbance, not a diagnosable illness, uncommon.
FT 0.50 s. CT rises from 0.60 to 0.70 s. Calculate and interpret. (3)
0.833 to 0.714, down 14.3%. Same output, longer to produce it: strategy change consistent with neuromuscular fatigue. Check protocol and normal variation first.
CWI protocol plus two mechanisms. (4)
10 to 15°C for 10 to 15 min. Hydrostatic pressure limits oedema. Cold causes vasoconstriction, slows nerve conduction (analgesia), raises parasympathetic activity.
Correct this: "post-match static stretching removes lactate and prevents DOMS". (2)
Stretching is not shown to clear lactate, lactate does not cause DOMS, and stretching has not consistently prevented DOMS or restored performance.
Explain potentiation and three programming rules. (5)
Brief intense effort raises later force or speed via more fast-twitch recruitment. Within 10% load, 6 to 12 min rest, trained athletes only, trial in training first.
List the eight crisis steps. (4)
Self-regulate, validate, summarise, open questions, get help, safety plan, end and contract, debrief.
Five problems with: "CK is high, ACWR is 1.20 and compression cut soreness, so they are overtrained but will be fine tomorrow." (5)
CK shows acute damage and is variable. ACWR is descriptive with poor prediction. OTS cannot be diagnosed from two numbers. Less soreness is not restored performance. No basis for a recovery deadline. No comparison to credit the socks. Context ignored.

Medium, 8 to 10 marks

Evaluate the ACWR for managing injury risk. (10)
Definition and fitness-fatigue origin. Appeal: training logic, guidelines, 130+ studies, association. Flaws: coupling (show 1.21 vs 1.30), rolling averages, ratio noise, poor prediction. Both high and low load raise risk, not all load equal. Verdict: cautious use with raw load change, response measures, clinician input.
Explain the stress bucket and apply it to a squad in a congested fixture block with travel. (8)
Bucket, stones, water, taps, overflow, three actions. Action plans in preseason, alternative taps for travel, link wellness flags to conversations, downtime before pinch points, cut inflow (meetings, media), staff model self-care.
Compare CWI, HWI and CWT for two matches 48 h apart. (10)
Shared pressure mechanism, different temperature effects. Protocols. Vaile 2008a and b, Lane and Wenger, Hamlin. HWI weakest, contraindicated with acute injury. Belief effect. Competition phase so maximise recovery. Recommend CWI or CWT with athlete choice.
Discuss the benefits and risks of warm-up. (8)
Temperature chain, psychological, long-term value. Karpovich, fatigue, stretch reflex, potentiation timing, games, stretching's weak injury link. Verdict with the chapter's evidence caveat.
Critically discuss biochemical markers for a professional team. (10)
CK, cortisol, testosterone, T:C, Gln:Glu, immune, cytokines: what each shows and its problem. Practical barriers. Table 8.2 "questionable". Better value from sRPE, AROMs, CMJ.
Two matches 24 h apart. Coach plans 30 min extra running "to clear lactate", a long compulsory treatment circuit and a 06:00 meeting. Evaluate. (8)
Running adds load, lactate clearance is not recovery. Circuit delays sleep. Meeting cuts sleep opportunity. Priorities: clinical check, food, fluid, sleep, one familiar modality at a recorded dose, recheck before next warm-up.

Long, 12 to 15 marks

Design a season-long load and response monitoring system for a 22-player university hockey squad on a small budget. Justify, give frequencies, analysis and reporting. (15)

Design and justify a pre-match warm-up and cool-down for a university rugby sevens side, 19:00 in winter, three games in a day. (15)
